Every video file is two things at once. The **container** is the file format you see in the
name, such as `.mp4`, `.mov` or `.webm`. Inside it, the picture is compressed with a **codec**,
and that choice decides how big the file is, how good it looks for its size, and which devices
can play it. Concat exports three: H.264, HEVC and AV1, always in an MP4.

## The short answer

| You are making                                   | Export as | Why                                                          |
| ------------------------------------------------ | --------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Anything you will upload: YouTube, TikTok, Reels | **H.264** | Every platform accepts it and re-encodes it on upload anyway |
| A file to send to someone, or for a client       | **H.264** | It plays on every phone, laptop and TV made in 15 years      |
| A file to keep on your own disk                  | **HEVC**  | About half the size of H.264 for the same look               |
| The smallest file at a given quality             | **AV1**   | Smaller again, if the machine can encode it                  |
| 10-bit or HDR footage you want to keep that way  | **HEVC**  | 10-bit H.264 plays almost nowhere                            |

When in doubt, H.264. It is the default for a reason.

## H.264, the one that plays everywhere

H.264 (also called AVC) is from 2003. Every browser, phone, smart TV, games console and editing
app decodes it, most of them in hardware, so it plays smoothly and uses little battery. Its
compression is the least efficient of the three: for the same picture it needs the most bits.

That rarely matters for uploads. YouTube, TikTok, Instagram and the rest decode whatever you send
and encode their own copies, so a well-made H.264 file at a generous bitrate is the best thing to
hand them. A smaller HEVC or AV1 upload does not come out looking better on the other side.

## HEVC, half the size

HEVC (H.265) arrived in 2013 and needs roughly half the bits of H.264 for the same quality, which
is what Concat's Export sheet means by "Half the size". iPhones record in it by default. It is
the sensible choice for your own archive, for long recordings and for 10-bit footage.

The catch is playback. Apple devices and recent Windows and Android hardware decode it, but not
every browser does on every machine, and some web services still refuse it. A file you send in
HEVC may not open on the other person's computer.

## AV1, the smallest

AV1 is from 2018 and was made by a group of companies including Google, Netflix and Mozilla, free of
the licence fees attached to HEVC. It compresses better again, which is why streaming services use
it. Recent browsers, phones and graphics cards decode it; older ones do not.

Encoding AV1 is heavy work. On Windows and Linux, Concat hands it to the graphics chip when the
machine has an AV1 encoder (recent NVIDIA, Intel and AMD graphics), ahead of the software encoder,
and the Export sheet shows "Not on this device" for a codec the machine cannot encode at all.

## Bitrate matters more than the codec

The codec decides how efficiently bits are spent; the **bitrate** decides how many there are. An
H.264 file at a sensible bitrate beats an AV1 file starved of bits. Concat's Export sheet starts
the bitrate at what YouTube asks for at the timeline's size and frame rate (8 Mbps at 1080p, 40 at
4K, half again above 30 fps, less for HEVC and AV1) and that is right for almost everything. The
[file size calculator](/tools/video-file-size-calculator) shows what a bitrate means in megabytes.

## Exporting in Concat

Open the Export sheet (the blue **Export** button, or <kbd class="kbd">Ctrl+E</kbd>,

<kbd class="kbd">⌘E</kbd> on a Mac) and pick the **Codec**: H.264 ("Plays everywhere"), HEVC ("Half
the size") or AV1 ("Smallest"). Turn on **10-bit colour** only for 10-bit or HDR footage. The
[export guide](/guides/export) covers every other row.

## Changing the codec of a file you already have

To turn an HEVC phone video into H.264 that plays anywhere, or a large file into a smaller one,
you do not need to open an editor: the [video converter](/tools/convert-video) and
[Compress a video](/tools/compress-video) do it in your browser, and the
[video file inspector](/tools/video-info) tells you which codec a file uses.
